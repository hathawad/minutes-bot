### A. The CarePortal Model

CarePortal is a "care sharing" technology connecting local churches with children and families in crisis so churches can respond to critical needs in real time. It operates as an ecosystem of three parts:

- **Requesting agencies.** Nonprofits, pregnancy centers, schools, and DCF submit vetted requests to the platform. The caseworker who knows the family's full situation enters the request.
- **Responding churches.** Only churches may meet a family in person. This is the core design principle of the program.
- **Community funders.** Businesses, organizations, and individuals may fund requests but may not meet families in person.

CJ framed the underlying philosophy: the aim is to care for children by caring for families, and the best vehicle for caring for families is the local church. Material provision is the entry point, with the goal of the church building an ongoing relationship, praying with the family, sharing the gospel, and inviting them into the church community.

**Context.** Miami-Dade is one of the roughly five percent of U.S. counties from which fifty percent of children in foster care originate. CarePortal works across the full spectrum: prevention (schools, where families face needs such as school clothing but are not at risk of losing children), prevention and preservation (agencies such as Citrus Family Care Network, where lacking basics such as bedding can itself become grounds for a DCF removal), children already in foster care, and those who have aged out.

**How a request flows.** A caseworker submits a vetted need. CarePortal alerts nearby churches by email and text. The responding church meets the family in person and provides the item.

**Common request types.** Cribs, beds, and furniture; groceries; diapers and wipes. Rent and utilities are deliberately excluded because the cost of living in Miami-Dade makes them overwhelming for churches.

**Program stats (Miami-Dade).** Roughly 5,000 children served, approaching $1 million in cumulative impact, and about 103 response teams across participating churches.

### B. Platform Walkthrough

**Fund versus card.** These are two distinct balances, and the distinction was the main point of confusion in discussion:

- The **CarePortal fund** is the church's pool of donated money.
- The **CarePortal card** is a reloadable debit card used to make purchases.

Money moves from the fund to the card only when the church responds to a request and commits to funding it. Whoever holds the card makes the purchase. A church may issue more than one card.

**OCPC balances as presented:** CarePortal card, $707. CarePortal fund, approximately $2,442.

**Public giving page.** Each church has a public CarePortal page where anyone may give to that church's fund without a login, and those funds can only be used by that church. This came up in response to a question about members, peripheral attenders, and non-members who follow the ministry and want to contribute.

**Radius settings.** OCPC's request radius is currently 15 miles. The connecting-church radius, governing how far the church will travel to meet a partially or fully funded request, is 30 miles. Both are adjustable.

**Team management.** A church may run multiple response teams, each with its own radius. CJ suggested this as a way to distribute notification load, for example a north team and a south team keyed to community group locations, rather than having two people receive every alert.

**Browsing and responding to requests.** Requests display urgency (normal means seven to ten days), general family location, submitting agency, household composition, language spoken, a narrative summary, and an estimated cost and item count. Churches select "Yes, I can help" and then choose to **fund** the request or **provide** the items. Requests can be filtered by county, submitting agency, tier, items requested, and zip code.

**Reporting.** Request history can be filtered, columns added, and the full set exported for reporting to church leadership: requests met, children served, locations, dates funded, and estimated value. After meeting a request, the church verifies it on the platform, which notifies the caseworker to close it out.

### C. Discussion and Questions

**Request detail can be thin.** A question was raised that a request reading "$300 / three items" is ambiguous, as it could mean three sets of school uniforms, three bags of clothing, or roughly $100 per child. CJ agreed that particular request was poorly written and confirmed that well-written requests specify exact items and sizes. In every case the responding church has direct access to the caseworker and should call for clarification. CJ offered to intervene with agencies where needed.

**Who can fund, and on whose behalf.** A question was raised about whether an individual could fund a request on the church's behalf. Any individual may fund a request from their own resources, and any individual may give to the church's CarePortal fund. Committing church funds to a specific request requires platform access, so that action runs through those already set up on OCPC's team. Meeting a family in person requires completed training regardless.

**Used items are acceptable.** Churches may fulfill a request with used furniture or clothing rather than spending fund money, subject to confirming with the family or caseworker. CJ noted most families are glad to receive them.

**Vetting.** Agencies, not CarePortal, vet the needs. The agency mix is deliberately broad and includes both Christian organizations (Glory House Miami, pregnancy centers) and secular ones (DCF, public schools). CarePortal trains agencies on submission standards and will push back on a request that appears illegitimate. CJ noted the platform also functions as a point of contact with local social workers.

**Family composition.** A question was raised about how household composition appears on requests and how the church should handle requests that do not fit its convictions. CJ explained that the agency specifies the household in the request, that the church always has the option not to respond to any given request, and that the caseworker is available for context before the church commits.

**Trafficking and acute cases.** A question was raised about how severe cases intersect with the platform, given that law enforcement and DCF are the first responders in those situations. CJ explained that CarePortal engages downstream: once children are recovered and triaged, they are placed with relatives or other caregivers, and those households often have immediate material needs. CarePortal asks to be kept informed of local placements so churches can come alongside those families.

**Tier 2 (relational requests) is in pilot.** Beyond material provision, CarePortal is piloting relational requests such as tutoring a struggling student or visiting an elderly person. Because these involve sustained contact with minors, background checks are required, and the feature has not launched. Under the current system tier 2 can only be surfaced as a filter, not routed to a dedicated team. CJ noted that relational follow-up already happens organically after a material request is met, and that follow-up outside the platform requires no CarePortal training.

### E. Training and Recruitment

Anyone who meets a family in person on behalf of the church must complete CarePortal's brief training, which covers approaching families who have often experienced significant trauma. Anyone following up relationally afterward does not need the training.

CJ observed that the most common profile on the platform is a single mother without relational support, and that the deepest need is usually not the item but a trustworthy relationship. He noted this is a natural fit for women in the church willing to meet with these mothers.

### B. Mercy Ministry: Mike Tunez

**Committee composition.** The mercy committee has been reconstituted as all-deacon following the June roster turnover. Thanks were given to Emmanuel Uche, Christopher Lilburn, and Michael Batts. The committee currently has four members. **Mike asked for a fifth volunteer**, primarily so the committee can break ties.

**Volume.** Requests have been steady, averaging one to two per week. Mike did not have the total disbursed at hand, estimating four to five thousand dollars, and will confirm the figure.

**Process.** The standard path is a written request form, submitted to the church office, which forwards it to Mike. Requests from church members are generally straightforward. Requests from outside the church are followed up with a call and vetting before action.

**Scope.** Recipients need not be church members. Most come through the church office; one recent case involved a non-member in another county. Several recipients have begun attending services, including one family and one individual attending consistently. Mike asked the diaconate to keep these people in prayer.

**Sustained assistance and dependency.** A concern was raised about assistance becoming enabling if recipients remain in the same circumstances afterward. Mike agreed this is a significant consideration, particularly for members. For a handful of people with ongoing legitimate need, notably elderly members facing rising costs, the committee has committed to recurring help, generally paired with an understanding of how the assistance is to be used. Where a recipient's circumstances are unstable, help is offered alongside an expectation that they take steps to stabilize. The committee is developing a more formal approach to this, which Mike described as new ground for OCPC and a topic for an upcoming committee discussion.

Mike also asked for advice from the group on encouraging gospel engagement with people who come for help without being heavy-handed.

### C. CarePortal: Mike Tunez, Christopher Lilburn, Iain Kelso

Mike gave the operational counterpart to CJ's presentation.

**Who is involved.** Mike Tunez, Christopher Lilburn, and Iain Kelso, with Charlotte DePaula, and Vanessa operating largely independently while staying connected to the team.

**The bottleneck is in-person delivery.** Funding requests is comparatively easy; physically delivering, assembling, and meeting families is the hard part. Steve Hendrikse did a great deal of this work, building and delivering furniture and getting the opportunity to meet and pray with recipients.

**Two-person rule.** Mike noted a current unwritten practice that **no one is sent to meet a recipient alone; at least two people go**, for accountability and visibility. He recommended the diaconate consider formalizing this.

**Volume and selection.** Request notifications run to dozens per week, enough that Mike created a separate email account for them. There is no way to meet them all. Selection is largely first-come, first-served after filtering out requests that appear unnecessary. In Mike's assessment, agency vetting is inconsistent; the majority of requests are legitimate, but some are not.

**Capacity.** Mike was candid that CarePortal runs second to core mercy work for his bandwidth, that the platform is not especially user friendly, and that steps get missed, including post-fulfillment verification. At $600 per month, OCPC can typically cover one to four requests, usually about two.

**Staffing question.** It was suggested the function may eventually warrant a full-time staff role. Mike's view is that with enough deacons sharing the load it remains manageable, since availability varies across the group and much of the work can be done on weekends or days off.

**Ask.** Mike asked deacons interested in getting involved to talk to him and he will get them set up. The required training runs about thirty minutes and is primarily platform orientation. He also noted he posts specific needs to the WhatsApp group when two people are needed for a delivery or assembly.
